THE HARBOUR


THE KEEPER

Edith Marlow came to Harwick on the last ferry of October, with one
trunk and a letter from the Lighthouse Board.

The harbour smelled of tar and rain. Captain Rowe met her on the quay
and took the trunk without asking.

“You will be the new keeper, then,” said Captain Rowe. “The last one
lasted a winter.”

“I mean to last longer than that,” said Edith.

They walked the cliff path in silence. Below them the sea worked at
the rocks the way a dog works at a bone.

The lighthouse stood at the end of the headland, white and narrow, its
lamp dark in the afternoon light.

Inside, the logbook lay open on the table. The last entry was dated in
March and ended in the middle of a word.

Edith read it twice, closed the book, and climbed the hundred and
twelve steps to the lamp room.

The lamp room was cold and smelled of paraffin. The great lens sat in
its brass cage like a sleeping animal.

She wound the clockwork that turned the lens, trimmed the wicks, and
polished the glass until her arms ached.

From the gallery she could see the whole of Harwick: the harbour, the
chapel, the row of white cottages along the quay.

Beyond the harbour the sea darkened toward the Shoals, where the water
broke white over rocks she could not see.

At dusk she lit the lamp. The beam swept out across the water, and
somewhere below a dog began to bark.

Captain Rowe had left a loaf and a jug of milk on the kitchen table.
She ate standing up, watching the light turn.

That night she slept in her clothes on the narrow bed below the lamp
room, and woke every two hours to wind the clockwork.

In the morning the logbook was still open at the unfinished entry, as
if the old keeper had only stepped out.


THE LOGBOOK

For a week Edith kept the lamp, wound the clockwork, and wrote the
weather in the logbook every four hours.

On the eighth day Martha Rowe, the captain’s sister, came up the path
with bread and a basket of eggs.

“People in Harwick talk,” said Martha. “They say the old keeper saw
lights out past the Shoals.”

Edith showed her the unfinished entry. Martha read it and went pale.
“He wrote that the night he left.”

That evening Edith searched the lamp room and found a second logbook
behind a loose board, wrapped in oilcloth.

The second logbook was full of numbers: times, bearings, and the same
three letters repeated, S. H. L.

She sat up late with the lamp turning above her, matching the bearings
to the chart of the Shoals.

Every bearing pointed to the same place, a rock the chart called the
Grey Widow.

She went down to the harbour the next morning and found Captain Rowe
mending nets outside his cottage.

“Who was the old keeper?” Edith asked. “Nobody has told me his name.”

“Samuel Hale,” said Captain Rowe, without looking up. “He kept the
light for nine years before he went.”

S. H. L., Edith thought. Samuel Hale, Lighthouse. He had been signing
his own name in the margins.

“Where did he go?” she asked. The captain put down his needle and
looked out toward the Shoals for a long time.

“Nobody knows,” he said at last. “His boat was found on the beach in
April. He was not in it.”
